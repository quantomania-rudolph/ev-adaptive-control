What the powertrain tells the data team

Every number below comes from the real simulation: a 3-phase permanent-magnet motor with a field-oriented controller. The motor is fed by a modelled battery pack and DC-link capacitor. At the shaded moment the battery management system cuts the allowed pack current. Switch the run to see what an unprotected controller does and what a protected one does, and watch what each channel records.

1. Watch the event travel through the powertrain

Press play or drag the slider. Each card is one stage of the chain from pack to road. Each box below is a request-versus-delivery gap at one hand-off, with the share that actually arrived. In the shaded window the BMS cuts the allowed pack current, and you can follow the loss from the first box to the last.

2. What the controller asked for, and what the motor got

This is the clipped saturation. The controller requests a voltage on the q axis (dashed). The inverter can only deliver up to the ceiling (orange). The solid line is what was applied. The shaded band is the unannounced sag. The scale stops at 260 V: where the dashed line leaves the top, the request is far larger (hover for the value).

3. The mismatch, step by step

Delivered minus requested, in volts, on both axes. Zero means the request was honoured. This is the M2 ledger row, logged every 100 µs, plus a second check against the plant's own reported voltage that proves nothing was clipped behind our back.

4. The disturbance vector d

The plant is written as ẋ = f(x) + g(x)u + d. Here d has three parts: the voltage the actuator failed to deliver, acting on each current, and the road load acting on speed. Hover to read values. Units are rates of change.

5. Energy requested but never delivered

The torque request implies a shaft power. The difference between that and the power the shaft actually produced, added up over time, is the energy the powertrain owed and did not pay. Before the event it stays near zero. Afterwards the protected run stops accumulating, and the unprotected one keeps going.

6. The natural process underneath

Back-EMF grows with speed and eats the voltage budget before the controller gets to use any of it. When the ceiling falls below the back-EMF plus the demand, the motor cannot follow its request.

7. The battery and DC link

The pack current limit is the physical cause of the event. When the inverter asks for more current than the BMS allows, the DC-link capacitor covers the gap for a few milliseconds, then the bus voltage collapses and takes the inverter ceiling with it.
